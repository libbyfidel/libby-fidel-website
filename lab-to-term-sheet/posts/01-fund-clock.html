Your raise runs on the fund's clock

Before you pitch a VC, understand the vehicle writing the check. Its structure and its age shape what it can say yes to.

6 min readInteractiveVenture · Fundamentals

Most technical founders treat "a VC" as a single decision-maker. It isn't. Each check comes from a fund: a pool of other people's money with a fixed lifespan and a fixed job, which is to return more than went in before time runs out.

That has real consequences for a deep-tech company. Your timeline to revenue is measured in years, sometimes many of them. If you don't know where a fund sits in its life, you can't tell whether your timeline fits it.

The 10-year clock

A typical fund is built to last about ten years, often with an option to extend by a year or two. The first several years, called the commitment period, are when the fund writes new checks. After that it can only support the companies it already owns. In the final stretch, the focus moves to getting money back through acquisitions, IPOs, or secondary sales.

What this means for deep tech

Hardware, materials, energy, and bio companies often need five or more years before the business looks like a business. That's fine early in a fund's life, when there's room for a long build. Pitch a fund in year four or five and the same company has to hit commercial milestones much faster, and the GP may push you harder than your technology can handle.

Before your first meeting, ask: "When did this fund close, and how much is left for new investments?"

It's a normal question, and good investors will answer it straight. Fund vintage and remaining reserves are usually easy to find, either in the firm's own announcements or in a quick diligence call with founders they've backed.

Takeaways
  • A VC check comes from a fund with a deadline, not from an open-ended pot.
  • New checks happen mostly in the first ~5 years; after that, funds back their existing portfolio.
  • For long-build companies, early-vintage funds are a much better fit.
