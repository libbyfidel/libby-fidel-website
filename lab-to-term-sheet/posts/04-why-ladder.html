You have an invention. Why do people care?

Most product-market-fit playbooks start with a customer. Deep-tech founders start with a breakthrough. Here's how to climb from one to the other.

7 min readInteractiveDeep tech · Product-market fit

The standard startup advice is to find a painful problem and then build the solution. That works well for software, which can be shaped into almost anything. It works less well when what you have is a new electrode chemistry, a sensor, or a better way to grow a crystal.

That doesn't mean technology-first is the wrong approach. One study of 137 US unicorns found that market-first, technology-first, and balanced strategies can all reach billion-dollar valuations. Market-first was the most common route. Technology-first was linked to the fastest growth.

The trick is to be disciplined about how you go from invention to market.

The ladder

Start with your research question at the bottom. Then keep asking one question: why do people care? Each answer is a rung, and each rung is a possible product with a different customer, scope, and market size. Climbing too high makes you a fuel company. Stopping too low may leave you selling a component nobody budgets for.

In the example, one research finding opens up three very different companies: an electrode supplier, an electrolyzer maker, or a green fuel producer. Each one has its own customers, capital needs, and competitors, and you can test product-market fit for each before you commit.

Is the problem actually urgent?

A rung is only worth building on if the people there feel the pain. A handy way to check: how important is the need, and how satisfied are people with what they use today? The opportunity is where the need is important and current options fall short.

Don't guess. Go ask.

Those two sliders should come from conversations, not from your own intuition. A few rules for customer discovery interviews:

  1. Ask about their priorities before you show them yours. Get them to list their top five to ten problems. If yours doesn't come up, it may not matter, however enthusiastic they are once they see your demo.
  2. Ask about behavior, not opinions. Are they spending money or headcount on this today, or just complaining about it?
  3. Map what they use now. How do they solve it today, and what do they love and hate about that?
  4. Look for patterns across interviews. One excited customer is an anecdote. Eight with the same workaround is a market.

If you have to ask whether you have product-market fit, you probably don't. When you do, customers pull the product out of your hands.

Takeaways
  • Technology-first is a valid path; just climb the ladder deliberately.
  • Every rung is a different company. Pick your scope on purpose.
  • Validate urgency through behavior in interviews, not compliments.
