Seventeen out of twenty will fail. That's the plan.

Venture math rests on one outlier carrying the whole portfolio. Once you see it, you'll understand every question a VC asks you.

5 min readInteractiveVenture · Portfolio math

Roughly nine out of ten startups fail. A bank can't lend into odds like that. Venture capital can, but only because it doesn't need most of its bets to work. It needs a very small number of them to work spectacularly.

This is the power law: returns are not spread evenly across a portfolio. They pile up in one or two companies. Here's a fund with twenty equal-sized checks. Click any company to change its outcome.

Try "No home run." Seventeen failures plus three respectable 5x outcomes gives a fund that loses money. Now try "All solid outcomes": twenty companies that each return 2x sounds safe, but it barely beats the stock market after ten years of locked-up capital and fees. LPs don't sign up for that.

The classic portfolio works because of one tile. Remove the 100x and the fund collapses.

It's also a volume game

To find that one tile, firms look at an enormous number of companies. A typical funnel looks something like this:

Companies seen ~1,000+
First meetings ~100–200
Deep diligence ~20–40
Investment committee ~10
Invested ~2–5

Labs and university tech-transfer offices can take the same approach: put many technologies through cheap, quick screens rather than going all in on one.

What this means when you pitch

A VC isn't asking "will this company succeed?" They're asking "if this works, could it be the one that returns the whole fund?" A great, profitable $80M business is a wonderful outcome for its founders, and a rounding error for a $200M fund.

Your job in the room is to make the 100x scenario believable, not just possible.

Takeaways
  • VCs expect most investments to fail; it's priced in.
  • A fund's result is usually decided by one or two companies.
  • So every pitch is judged by its upside, not just its odds of survival.
